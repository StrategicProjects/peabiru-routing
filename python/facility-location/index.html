    Facility location¶

    Travel times say how well a place is served today. locate_facilities() turns the question around: given the people to serve and the places where a facility could open, where should the next ones go? floating_catchment() measures the other side of access, supply against the demand that competes for it. Both work on the travel times of the network, by any mode, and both are peabiru's own functions.

    The four models¶

    locate_facilities() takes the demand points (with a weight each: residents, pupils, patients), the candidate sites and, optionally, the facilities that already exist, and solves one of four classic models:

    model question objective
    "max_cover" with n_facilities new facilities, how many people can be within cutoff minutes of one? demand covered (to maximise)
    "set_cover" how few facilities put everyone within cutoff minutes? number of new sites
    "p_median" where do n_facilities facilities make the average trip shortest? demand-weighted minutes to the nearest facility
    "p_center" where do they make the worst trip shortest? minutes of the demand point farthest from its nearest facility

    A demand point is covered by a site at most cutoff minutes away (the cutoff included). The existing facilities count first: only the demand they leave uncovered, or the travel time they leave, drives the choice of the new sites, and they are never closed or moved.

    A worked example: two new schools¶

    The sample network has 27 points with their population; six of them have a school. Where would two more schools put the most residents within a 12-minute walk of one?

    import peabiru

network = peabiru.build_network(peabiru.sample_data_path(), n_threads=2)
points = peabiru.sample_points()
schools = points[points["schools"] > 0]
others = points[points["schools"] == 0]

found = peabiru.locate_facilities(
    network, points, others, existing=schools,
    model="max_cover", n_facilities=2, cutoff=12,
    demand_colname="population", mode="WALK", n_threads=2,
)
print(found.sites[["id", "order", "marginal", "cumulative"]].to_string(index=False))

                 id  order  marginal  cumulative
89a8100c03bffff      1    5658.0     43534.0
89a8100c383ffff      2    2683.0     46217.0

    The result has four parts. sites lists the new sites by what each adds: marginal is the demand it newly covers and cumulative the demand covered so far, existing schools included. summary has the totals:

    s = found.summary
print(f"covered today:  {s['covered_before']:.0f} of {s['total_demand']:.0f} residents "
      f"({100 * s['covered_before'] / s['total_demand']:.1f}%)")
print(f"greedy:         {s['objective_greedy']:.0f}")
print(f"after {s['n_swaps']} swap:   {s['covered']:.0f} "
      f"({100 * s['covered'] / s['total_demand']:.1f}%)")

    covered today:  37876 of 46273 residents (81.9%)
greedy:         46040
after 1 swap:   46217 (99.9%)

    candidates has one row per candidate with its potential: the residents not covered today that a school there would cover alone. It is the map to draw before choosing anything, since it shows every good place and not only the winners:

    best = found.candidates.sort_values("potential", ascending=False).head(4)
print(best[["id", "potential", "chosen"]].to_string(index=False))

                 id  potential  chosen
89a8100c033ffff     5764.0   False
89a8100c03bffff     5658.0    True
89a8100c007ffff     4829.0   False
89a8100c00fffff     3936.0   False

    The candidate with the largest potential is not in the solution: the two sites chosen overlap less with each other than it does with either. That is the difference between ranking sites one by one and choosing a set.

    demand has one row per demand point: its weight, the nearest open facility (existing or new), the minutes to it and whether it is covered.

    How the models are solved¶

    By heuristics, with no integer programming solver:

    1. Greedy construction. Sites are opened one at a time, each the one that improves the objective most given the ones already open. For maximal covering this covers at least 63% (1 - 1/e) of the most that any choice of n_facilities sites could cover of the demand not covered today (Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher, 1978); in practice it is usually much closer.
    2. Vertex substitution (swap=True, the default; Teitz and Bart, 1968). An open new site is swapped for a closed candidate while that improves the objective, taking the best swap of all at each step. The result is never worse than the greedy one, and summary reports both (objective_greedy, objective, n_swaps).

    In the example the greedy pair covers 46,040 residents and one swap takes it to 46,217, which is the optimum: an exhaustive search over the 210 pairs of candidates finds nothing better. That is not guaranteed in general; the solutions are good and reproducible, not proven optimal.

    Details by model:

    • "set_cover" opens the site that covers the most demand points not yet covered until every point some site can cover is covered, then drops the sites the others make redundant.
    • "p_center" compares the largest travel time and, on ties, the demand-weighted sum, in the greedy step and in the swaps.
    • In "set_cover" and "p_center" a point with weight 0 is not demand, and the others count the same whatever their weight.
    • In "p_median" and "p_center", a demand point that reaches no open facility within max_trip_duration is charged unreachable_time minutes (by default twice the longest travel time of the table), so that serving a point always beats shortening the trip of another. A point that no site at all can serve is left out of the objective and reported in summary["unservable_demand"].
    • Ties go to the candidate listed first, and the result does not depend on n_threads.

    The other models¶

    How few schools would put every resident within a 10-minute walk, starting from none?

    cover = peabiru.locate_facilities(
    network, points, points, model="set_cover", cutoff=10,
    demand_colname="population", mode="WALK", n_threads=2,
)
print(len(cover.sites), "sites;", cover.summary["unservable_demand"], "residents out of reach")

    7 sites; 0.0 residents out of reach

    Two sites that make the average walk shortest, and two that make the longest walk shortest:

    for model in ("p_median", "p_center"):
    r = peabiru.locate_facilities(
        network, points, points, model=model, n_facilities=2,
        demand_colname="population", mode="WALK", max_trip_duration=60, n_threads=2,
    )
    mean = (r.demand["demand"] * r.demand["travel_time"]).sum() / r.demand["demand"].sum()
    print(f"{model}: {', '.join(r.sites['id'])}; "
          f"mean {mean:.1f} min, longest {r.demand['travel_time'].max():.0f} min")

    p_median: 89a8100c027ffff, 89a8100c03bffff; mean 9.7 min, longest 32 min
p_center: 89a8100c02fffff, 89a8100c067ffff; mean 13.3 min, longest 19 min

    The p-median pair gives the shorter average and the p-center pair the shorter worst case: the two models answer different questions.

    Any mode works, since the travel times come from travel_time_matrix(): pass mode, departure_datetime and any other routing argument.

    import datetime as dt

transit = peabiru.locate_facilities(
    network, points, points, model="p_median", n_facilities=3,
    demand_colname="population", mode=["WALK", "TRANSIT"],
    departure_datetime=dt.datetime(2019, 5, 13, 14, 0), seed=1,
    max_trip_duration=60, n_threads=2,
)
print(list(transit.sites["id"]))

    ['89a8100c03bffff', '89a8100c1dbffff', '89a8100c15bffff']

    Supply against demand: the floating catchment area¶

    Being near a school is not the same as having a place in it. floating_catchment() computes the two-step floating catchment area (Luo and Wang, 2003):

    1. each facility's supply is divided by the demand within its catchment;
    2. each demand point adds up the ratios of the facilities within its catchment.

    The result is supply per unit of demand at each demand point: here, schools per resident within a 20-minute walk.

    fca = peabiru.floating_catchment(
    network, points, schools, cutoff=20,
    demand_colname="population", supply_colname="schools", mode="WALK", n_threads=2,
)
print(fca.supply[["id", "supply", "catchment_demand"]].to_string(index=False))
per_10k = 10_000 * fca.demand["accessibility"]
print(f"schools per 10,000 residents: from {per_10k.min():.2f} to {per_10k.max():.2f}")
print(f"shared out: {(fca.demand['demand'] * fca.demand['accessibility']).sum():.1f} schools")

                 id  supply  catchment_demand
89a8100c077ffff     1.0           22033.0
89a8100c1dbffff     2.0           17501.0
89a8100c39bffff     1.0           10593.0
89a8100c1cbffff     1.0           21263.0
89a8100c153ffff     1.0           22664.0

    The index shares out the supply: the sum over the demand points of demand times accessibility is the supply of the facilities that have any demand in their catchment.

    With decay_function="step" a pair counts in full up to cutoff minutes and not at all beyond. Any other decay function gives the enhanced form (Luo and Qi, 2009), in which nearer pairs weigh more, in both steps. The functions and their decay_value are those of accessibility(); the weight falls continuously with travel time, where Luo and Qi used a few travel-time zones.

    smooth = peabiru.floating_catchment(
    network, points, schools, cutoff=20, decay_function="logistic", decay_value=5,
    demand_colname="population", supply_colname="schools", mode="WALK", n_threads=2,
)
per_10k = 10_000 * smooth.demand["accessibility"]
print(f"schools per 10,000 residents: from {per_10k.min():.2f} to {per_10k.max():.2f}")

    schools per 10,000 residents: from 0.69 to 2.74

    From a table of travel times¶

    Both functions take a table of travel times instead of a network: from_id (demand point), to_id (site) and the minutes in travel_time_pXX or travel_time, as travel_time_matrix() returns it. A pair that is absent is unreachable. The points then need only their id, so the same matrix can serve several models, or travel times from another source can be used.

    import pandas as pd

sites = pd.concat([schools, others])
times = peabiru.travel_time_matrix(
    network, points, sites, mode="WALK", max_trip_duration=12, n_threads=2,
)
again = peabiru.locate_facilities(
    None, points[["id", "population"]], others[["id"]], existing=schools[["id"]],
    model="max_cover", n_facilities=2, cutoff=12, demand_colname="population",
    travel_times=times,
)
print(list(again.sites["id"]) == list(found.sites["id"]))

    True

    What the models do not see¶

    The models choose sites by travel time and demand alone. They do not know the capacity or the cost of a facility, whether land is available at a candidate site, the demand that comes from outside the study area, or how well the people already covered are served. Maximal covering treats 11 minutes and 1 minute alike, and p-median may leave a few people very far to shorten the average: comparing two models on the same data is often more informative than either alone.
